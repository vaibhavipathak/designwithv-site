Case study · Information architecture

KM Modernization

From content repository to decision support: a legacy knowledge base redesigned so frontline agents reach the right answer in seconds. Planned end to end and proven in a pilot.

Role
Knowledge Management Lead and information architect
Methods
Agent interviews, call shadowing, surveys, open card sorting, tree testing, affinity mapping, usage and call analytics
Status
Research, design and pilot completed. Full rollout planned, then halted by a company acquisition.
Tools
SharePoint, Confluence, Lucidchart, Figma, SurveyMonkey, Optimal Workshop, Tableau, Genesys and SpeechMiner

01 · The problem

Agents were guessing

A high-volume loyalty contact centre relied on a knowledge base built on a legacy SharePoint 2010 platform, organized by team name.

Core frontline agents mostly worked in one section, but the domain-specific and expert teams, who handle specialized processes, exceptions and escalations, worked across several. They had to remember which team owned each process, open that team's section, and back out to try another when they guessed wrong, all mid-call while the customer waited.

Research with frontline agents surfaced six themes:

  • Findability breakdowns
  • Cognitive load
  • Time pressure
  • Content fragmentation
  • Workarounds
  • Trust and confidence

02 · The approach

Restructure, not rewrite

  1. Turned a migration into a redesign

    A move to a new platform was already being discussed. Rather than carry the old structure across as it was, I pitched using the moment to rebuild it. The content was not the problem. Its structure was.

  2. Replaced team-based navigation with a permission-based model

    Every agent sees the same main navigation, organized by task. What appears under each heading depends on their access level, and the levels build on each other:

    • General access (core frontline teams) sees the general pages.
    • Specialized access (domain-specific teams) sees the general pages plus specialized ones.
    • Advanced access (expert teams handling exceptions and escalations) sees everything above plus their own processes, which no other team sees.

    Agents no longer need to remember which team owns a process: everything their role covers is in one place. And because every agent sees the same main navigation, a frontline agent moving into a specialized or expert role keeps working with labels they already know. Only the pages underneath grow.

  1. Designed a task-first IA

    Research showed agents think in terms of the action they need to take, not the team that owns it, so most labels are written as verbs: Update Profile, Book Travel, Resolve Travel Exceptions. I replaced deep hierarchies with a wide, shallow structure, so major tasks are reachable in three clicks or fewer, and front-loaded every label with its key word so agents can scan a list at a glance. Both changes reduce cognitive load and help agents answer faster mid-call.

  2. Built a metadata and content model

    One topic per page, consistent templates and shared labels, plus an owner and a review date for every page.

  3. Piloted where it was hardest

    The new structure went first to Travel Support, the back-office team that handled invoicing, so every new and changed booking passed through them. If it worked under that pressure, it would work for everyone.

03 · The outcome

Proven in pilot, planned for scale

4.3 → 2

clicks to reach a topic

~50 sec

saved per lookup

~$22,500

saved a year by the 16-agent pilot team, roughly 1,100 hours

$100K+

projected yearly savings at full scale, if each lookup saved just one click

In the Travel Support pilot, navigation guesswork disappeared, and leaders asked for the same landing-page pattern in every other section. The full rollout was planned and costed: across all contact-centre teams, saving just one click per lookup would save more than $100K a year. The pilot saved more than two clicks per lookup, so that projection is conservative. A company acquisition ended the program before it could be implemented.
