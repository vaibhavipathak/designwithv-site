The full story

01 The ChallengeCopilot was rolled out. Almost no one used it.

The situation: Brightline Support, a fictional contact centre, gave its agents Microsoft Copilot. Three months later, only a few enthusiasts used it each week. The goal had been broad: "use AI to work smarter."

Why usage stalled (from discovery)

  • Fear: "Will this replace me?" and "Will I get in trouble if it's wrong?"
  • Unclear rules: no one knew what customer details were safe to share, so most avoided AI and a few used personal tools in secret.
  • No clear payoff: generic demos didn't match the calls agents handle every day.
  • No reinforcement: managers weren't using it either.

The real problem: a change gap, not just a training gap. People needed a reason, permission and support to change habits, not only a course. I planned the fixes with the ADKAR change model (see Rollout).

What leadership asked me: "Why isn't anyone using it, and where should we focus?"

02 DiscoveryI asked agents what slows them down before designing anything.

03 Choosing the ApproachNo single model fit, so I matched each part of the project to the right one.

In one sentence: I used action mapping to decide what to build, SAM to build it quickly with feedback, and matched each lesson's method to the skill being learned.

What to build

Action mapping

  • Starts from the business goal and asks why people aren't doing each action.
  • Problems training can't fix (like unclear data rules) get fixed another way (clear rules, an AI assistant), so the course stays focused.

How to build

SAM, 3 rounds

  • Short prototype-test-revise rounds, so the course isn't outdated by launch and agents shape the design early.
  • Why not ADDIE: too linear for tools that change this fast.

How people learn each skill

SkillMethodWhy
Safe-use rulesBehaviourismClear right answers; quiz with instant feedback
Judging AI draftsConstructivismJudgment grows by working real cases
Daily AI habitsSocial learningPeople copy peers and champions

How each lesson flows

  • Gagné's nine events: from a hook to a job aid you keep.
  • Merrill's Pebble in the Pond: cases go from easy to hard, ending with a full real task.

Handling constant change

  • Content changes from yearly to several times a day, so fast-changing facts stay out of the course.
  • The course teaches lasting skills. Facts live in one source (a governed knowledge base and job aids), updated once and flagged by the weekly freshness check (see Knowledge Governance).

04 Designing the SolutionA five-step learning pathway, ordered from safest and easiest to hardest.

The learning pathway

  1. 1

    Why AI, and the safe-use rules

    Micro-lesson + quiz · 10 min

    Safety first: rules come before anyone touches customer data

  2. 2

    Asking the AI assistant

    Demo + practice · 10 min

    Quick, low-risk win; answers show their source, which builds trust

  3. 3

    Checking and editing AI call summaries

    Scenario eLearning · 20 min

    Needs judgment; reuses the source-checking habit from step 2

  4. 4

    Drafting customer emails

    Live virtual workshop · 45 min

    Hardest: tone and customer impact are best practised with coaching

  5. 5

    Becoming a champion (optional)

    Peer coaching guide

    For early adopters who'll support others

Why this order: safety first, a quick win next to build confidence, then harder judgment calls. Each step builds on the one before, in a blended mix of self-paced lessons, a live workshop and on-the-job support.

How I picked each format: how complex the skill is, how risky a mistake is, how often it changes, and whether it's needed mid-call.

Every objective has matching practice and proof

Agents will…PracticeProof
Apply the safe-use rulesSpot personal details in 5 sample notes5/5 correct
Use the AI assistant and verify its sourceTimed lookup challengeCorrect answer and source in under 30 sec
Evaluate an AI call summary4 branching cases, easy to hard80% scenario score
Create a customer email with AIWorkshop practice with the prompt cardsRubric-scored email

Designed for everyone

  • WCAG 2.2 AA: captions, alt text, contrast, keyboard navigation, screen-reader tested.
  • Plain language at about a grade 8 level.
  • Diverse names, accents and customer situations.
  • A short lesson on spotting bias in AI answers.
  • Workshop recorded with captions for anyone who can't attend live.

06 The AI AssistantAgents were searching 3+ places for answers, so I recommended an AI assistant and trained them to use it.

What agents get

  • Answers from a governed knowledge base only, with the source shown every time
  • "I don't know, ask your team lead" when the answer isn't in the content
  • A quiz mode to practise the safe-use rules

How agents learn to use it (pathway step 2, 10 minutes)

SkillHow it's taught
Ask clear questionsDemo: a vague question vs a specific one
Check the source before telling the customerPractice: spot an answer whose source doesn't match
Know when to escalatePractice: the assistant says "I don't know"; what next?
Keep personal data outReinforces the step 1 rules

Proof: a timed lookup challenge, with the correct answer and source in under 30 seconds (goal: from 90 to 30 seconds, which shortens handle time).

Trust built in: a "last reviewed" date on every source, and thumbs up/down feedback that goes to the content owner.

If a target is missed, the action map tells me where to look

  • High quiz scores but low usage: an environment problem (time, manager support), not a training gap, so the fix is outside the course.
  • Low quiz or scenario scores: a training gap, so revise that step and re-test.
  • Lookup time not improving: check the assistant's content for gaps, using agents' thumbs-down feedback.

Next phase: add the use cases champions report most often, and repeat the same cycle.

09 Knowledge GovernanceKnowledge lives in one governed source, with owners, review cycles and a scheduled check.

Matched to how often things change

How often it changesExamplesWhere it livesHow it stays current
Daily, even hourlyPromotions, outages, policy exceptionsOne source: the knowledge base and job aidsUpdated once at the source by its owner; never repeated in the course
Monthly to quarterlyAI tool features, prompt examplesJob aids, demo screensMonthly check of vendor release notes
YearlySafe-use rules, core skillsCourse modulesAnnual review, or sooner if rules change

A scheduled freshness check

An automated weekly task, set up as a Copilot agent or a Claude skill.

  • Flags any page past its review date (90 days)
  • Lists new AI tool updates that may affect job aids or demos
  • Collects thumbs-down feedback from agents
  • Sends one short digest to the content owner

Humans stay in charge: the check only flags and drafts; the content owner approves every change.

Knowledge governance built in

  • Owners and review dates: every article shows its owner and last-reviewed date.
  • Article template: answer first, then steps, then source, so articles are easy to scan and update.
  • Taxonomy and metadata: articles tagged by product, policy type and audience, so agents and the assistant find the right one.
  • KCS-style updates: agents flag gaps or errors while helping customers; owners fix them at the source.
  • Change log: what changed, when and why.

Repeatable playbooks

Each repeatable step became a written playbook (instructions, template, checklist), set up as a Copilot agent or a Claude skill, so the next program starts faster:

  • Discovery synthesizer
  • Use-case scorer
  • Format picker
  • Alignment checker
  • Accessibility and inclusion reviewer
  • Freshness reviewer

Ground rules I followed

  • No personal or company data in prompts.
  • Every AI instruction says: never invent information; if you don't know, say so clearly.
  • I reviewed every AI output before using it.
  • AI drafts; people decide. The same rules I taught the agents.
